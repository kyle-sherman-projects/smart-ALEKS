RCE overview

This brief evaluates ALEKS, an adaptive math program, at Southeast Polk Middle School during the 2025–26 school year. The analysis covers 572 Grade 6 students and asks one question: did students who spent more time on ALEKS score higher on the mid-year FastBridge aMath assessment? The design is correlational — it controls for school and grade level but doesn't assign students to treatment and control groups — which supports an ESSA Level III, Promising Evidence, claim. This is the first Research Brief for this engagement, so it serves as the baseline for future year-over-year comparison.

Flagged patterns & anomalies
These patterns surfaced from scanning across every subgroup and usage metric at once, not from reading each subgroup in isolation. Each states what the data shows, why it stands out, and the question it raises — never a causal claim.
1
The strongest gains are concentrated among the lowest-performing students (equity signal)

The two lowest prior-performance bands recorded the strongest effect sizes in the entire dataset.

Group 1 (lowest): r = +0.29, n = 113, significant Group 2: r = +0.32, n = 113, significant Group 5 (highest): r = -0.13, not significant

This runs against a "widening the gap" story. The students who started furthest behind show the clearest, most reliable gains, while the highest performers show no measurable benefit and a small negative trend.

Is ALEKS's adaptive difficulty especially well-matched to students in the lowest 40% of prior performance? And is the flat-to-negative trend among the highest performers a ceiling effect worth checking against content depth for that group?

2
Special Education students show both the lowest usage and no measurable effect

Special Education students averaged the least time on ALEKS of any subgroup and met the dosage goal at less than half the rate of General Education peers.

332 avg minutes (vs. 507 for Gen. Ed) 14% met dosage goal (vs. 30%) r = 0.00, not significant, n = 71

This is a compounding gap, not a single flag — low usage and no measurable outcome benefit appear together for the same subgroup. It isn't possible to tell from this data alone whether ALEKS doesn't work for these students or whether they simply haven't used it enough to show an effect.

What's driving the usage gap for Special Education students — access, scheduling, task design, or something else — and would outcomes look different if usage rose to match General Education levels?

3
High usage doesn't always mean a measurable effect

District-wide, the significant overall effect held even though just 28% of students met the dosage goal. But Black or African American students already average 690 minutes — above the goal — yet show no significant effect.

District-wide: 28% dosage, still significant Black/African American: 690 min used, r = +0.12, not significant, n = 73

This is the reverse of the district-wide pattern. For most students, the constraint looks like usage. For this subgroup, usage doesn't appear to be the constraint, so something else may be limiting measurable impact.

If dosage compliance rose district-wide toward the goal, how much additional benefit might the district see — and for the subgroup already meeting that goal without a measurable gain, what else might explain the gap: content fit, pacing, or assessment alignment?

Appendix D — Data notes & flags
  • Enrollment denominator not traceable. The "97% active" figure implies roughly 569 enrolled students, a number not present in any of the four source files reviewed for this brief — it likely traces to the original source PDF, which isn't included in this repository.
  • Single product, single site. This dataset covers only ALEKS at one school, so cross-product and cross-site anomaly checks don't apply here.
  • No prior brief available. This is the first Research Brief in this repository for this district, so year-over-year comparison wasn't possible. This report is the baseline going forward.
  • Small-n ethnicity subgroups. American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian, and Native Hawaiian/Pacific Islander subgroups all fell below the n = 30 threshold for a stable effect-size estimate and are reported for usage only, not effect.
  • Unrelated dataset excluded. contributors/amanda/aleks_table_mock.json (a Grades 3–5, treatment/control dataset) doesn't share a school, grade band, or study design with the data used in this brief, and was excluded rather than blended in.
