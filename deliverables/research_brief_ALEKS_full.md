# ALEKS outcomes analysis — Rapid Cycle Evaluation summary for Sample School District

**Author:** Amanda Cadran, PhD
**Reporting period:** August 25, 2025 – February 20, 2026 (Beginning of Year–Middle of Year)
**Grade level:** Grade 6
**School:** Southeast Polk Middle School
**Outcome measure:** FastBridge aMath (Middle of Year)
**ESSA evidence tier:** Level III — Promising Evidence
**Data baseline:** This is the first Research Brief for this engagement. No prior brief exists yet, so this report is the baseline for future year-over-year comparison.

---

## RCE overview

This brief evaluates ALEKS, an adaptive math program, at Southeast Polk Middle School during the 2025–26 school year. The analysis covers 572 Grade 6 students and asks one question: did students who spent more time on ALEKS score higher on the mid-year FastBridge aMath assessment? The design is correlational — it controls for school and grade level but doesn't assign students to treatment and control groups — which supports an ESSA Level III, Promising Evidence, claim.

## Summary of impacts

Grade 6 students who spent more time on ALEKS tended to score higher on the mid-year FastBridge aMath assessment. The overall effect size is +0.20 (a partial correlation, holding school and grade level constant), and the ninety-five percent confidence interval — the range we're confident the true effect falls within — runs from 0.12 to 0.28. Because that range doesn't cross zero, the result is statistically significant, meaning it's unlikely to be due to chance. A correlation of this size is considered small-to-moderate but practically meaningful in education research.

Most subgroups showed a positive relationship in the same direction as the overall finding. The strongest associations appeared among students in the lowest two prior-performance bands and among White and multiracial students. But usage tells a different story: only 28.26% of students met the recommended dosage of 665 minutes (about thirty minutes a week) over the twenty-five-week window, even though 97% of students logged into ALEKS at least once. Most of the sample used the product below the level associated with the strongest effects.

## Flagged patterns & anomalies

The patterns below surfaced from scanning across every subgroup and usage metric at once, not from reading each subgroup in isolation. Each one states what the data shows, why it stands out, and the question it raises for the district. None of these are causal claims — the data can't support that — so we've posed each as a question rather than a conclusion.

### 1. Strongest gains are concentrated among the lowest-performing students (equity signal)

**What the data shows:** the two lowest prior-performance bands recorded the strongest effect sizes in the entire dataset — Group 1 (lowest) at r = +0.29 (95% CI: 0.11–0.46, n = 113, significant) and Group 2 at r = +0.32 (95% CI: 0.14–0.48, n = 113, significant). Group 3 and Group 4 were positive but not significant, and Group 5 (highest prior performance) trended negative at r = -0.13 (95% CI: -0.30–0.06, not significant).

**Why it stands out:** this is the opposite of what a strict "widening the gap" story would predict. The students who started furthest behind show the clearest, most reliable gains — while the highest performers show no measurable benefit and a small negative trend.

**The question it raises:** is ALEKS's adaptive difficulty especially well-matched to students in the lowest forty percent of prior performance? And is the flat-to-negative trend among the highest performers a ceiling effect — students who've already mastered grade-level content getting less out of the adaptive path — worth checking against classroom placement or content depth for that group?

### 2. Special education students show both the lowest usage and no measurable effect

**What the data shows:** students with a Special Education designation averaged only 332 minutes on ALEKS (the lowest of any subgroup, versus 507 for General Education students) and met the dosage goal at less than half the rate of General Education peers (13.64% vs. 30.25%). Their effect size was r = 0.00 (95% CI: -0.25–0.25, n = 71, not significant) — a flat line, compared to a significant +0.21 for General Education students.

**Why it stands out:** this is a compounding gap, not a single flag. Low usage and no measurable outcome benefit appear together for the same subgroup, so it's not possible to tell from this data alone whether ALEKS doesn't work for these students or whether they simply haven't used it enough to show an effect.

**The question it raises:** what's driving the usage gap for Special Education students — access, scheduling, task design, or something else — and would outcomes look different if usage rose to match General Education levels?

### 3. District-wide, the product works even though most students fall short of the dosage goal

**What the data shows:** the significant, positive overall effect (r = +0.20) held even though just 28.26% of students met the 665-minute dosage goal, and 97% of students used ALEKS at some level.

**Why it stands out:** this is a high-impact, low-usage pattern. The constraint on stronger results doesn't appear to be whether the tool works — it appears to be how much of it students are getting.

**The question it raises:** if dosage compliance rose from 28% toward the recommended goal, how much additional benefit might the district see, and what would it take operationally to close that gap?

### 4. Black or African American students used ALEKS above the dosage goal but showed no significant effect

**What the data shows:** Black or African American students averaged 690 minutes on ALEKS — above the 665-minute goal, and the second-highest usage of any ethnicity subgroup — yet their effect size was r = +0.12 (95% CI: -0.13–0.36, n = 73), not statistically significant.

**Why it stands out:** this runs counter to the district-wide usage–impact pattern in flag 3. Here, usage is high but the measurable benefit isn't showing up, which is the opposite pairing from what the overall dosage story would predict.

**The question it raises:** given that usage doesn't appear to be the constraint for this subgroup, what else might explain why time on ALEKS hasn't translated into a measurable score gain — content fit, pacing, or something in how the assessment aligns with what students are practicing?

### 5. Three small ethnicity subgroups can't be evaluated for effect, despite unusually high dosage compliance

**What the data shows:** American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander students all had sample sizes below thirty (two, fourteen, and four students meeting the dosage goal out of small bases), so no stable effect size could be calculated. Two of these groups — American Indian or Alaska Native and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander — met the dosage goal at a fifty percent rate, well above the district average of 28.26%.

**Why it stands out:** these are the highest dosage-compliance rates in the entire dataset, but the group sizes are too small to say anything about whether that usage translated into outcomes.

**The question it raises:** as enrollment grows or data accumulates across additional periods, will these subgroups reach a sample size large enough to generate a reliable effect estimate?

## Next steps — discussion agenda

Ranked by how directly the district's own numbers raise each question.

1. **Dosage compliance.** Only 28.26% of students met the recommended dosage, yet the overall effect is already significant. What would it take to raise usage toward the 665-minute goal, and which groups would benefit most from that push? *(Flag 3)*
2. **Special education access and usage.** Special Education students show both the lowest usage and no measurable effect. What's contributing to the usage gap, and is it worth revisiting before drawing conclusions about whether ALEKS works for this group? *(Flag 2)*
3. **Lowest-performing students showing the strongest gains.** The two lowest prior-performance bands show the clearest, most reliable effects in the dataset. Is there something about how these students are using ALEKS — or how it's being introduced to them — worth replicating with other groups? *(Flag 1)*
4. **High usage without a measurable effect for Black or African American students.** This subgroup already exceeds the dosage goal but shows no significant effect. If usage isn't the constraint here, what else might be? *(Flag 4)*
5. **Small-sample ethnicity subgroups.** Three ethnicity subgroups are too small to evaluate for effect despite high dosage-compliance rates. Is there a plan to keep tracking these subgroups as sample sizes grow? *(Flag 5)*
6. **Highest-performing students trending flat to negative.** Group 5 is the only band trending negative, though not significantly. Is grade-level ALEKS content appropriately challenging for students who've already mastered the material? *(Flag 1)*

### Appendix A — ESSA evidence tiers

| Tier | Label | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| Level I | Strong Evidence | At least one well-designed randomized controlled study |
| Level II | Moderate Evidence | At least one well-designed quasi-experimental study |
| Level III | Promising Evidence | At least one well-designed correlational study with statistical controls for selection bias |
| Level IV | Demonstrates a Rationale | A well-specified logic model, with ongoing efforts to study effects |

This brief's Level III classification reflects a correlational design with statistical controls (school and grade level), not a randomized or quasi-experimental comparison.

### Appendix D — Data notes & flags

- **Enrollment denominator not traceable.** The brief's "97% of students used ALEKS" figure implies an enrolled-student count of roughly 569, which isn't present in any of the source data files reviewed for this brief (`subgroup-table-with-effect-size-details.json`, `average-usage-by-subgroup.json`, `percent-meeting-fidelity-subrgroup.json`, `student-demographic-table.json`). This figure is carried forward from the prior version of this brief but couldn't be independently re-verified from the data on hand.
- **Single product, single site.** This dataset covers only ALEKS at one school, so the cross-product and cross-site anomaly checks in the standard review procedure (same-school divergence across products, cross-product patterns by grade) don't apply here. Only usage-vs-impact, equity/subgroup, and threshold checks were run.
- **No prior brief available.** This is the first Research Brief in this repository for this district, so year-over-year comparison wasn't possible. This report is the baseline going forward.
- **Small-n ethnicity subgroups.** American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander subgroups all fell below the n = 30 threshold for a stable effect-size estimate and are reported for usage only, not effect.
- **Unrelated dataset excluded.** `contributors/amanda/aleks_table_mock.json` (a Grades 3–5, treatment/control dataset) doesn't share a school, grade band, or study design with the data used in this brief, and was excluded rather than blended in.
