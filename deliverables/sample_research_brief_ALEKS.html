Summary
What works, for whom, and under what conditions
Grade 6 students who spent more time using Smart Math 1,2,3 tended to score higher on the mid-year math assessment. Across all 572 students, that relationship was positive and statistically significant2 — but it was not the same for every group of students, and the single district-wide result hides most of what matters.
What works
Overall, more time using the product was associated with higher math scores, and that relationship is statistically significant — meaning it is unlikely to be a chance result. It meets the ESSA Level III "Promising Evidence" standard.5 This is an association, not proof that the product caused the higher scores.6
For whom
The relationship was statistically significant for students in the lowest 40% of prior performance9. For the highest-performing students it was not statistically significant, so no effect can be claimed in either direction. For Special Education students, no relationship was measurable at all.
Under what conditions
97% of students used the product at least once, but only 27% reached the recommended 665 minutes. Nearly all findings therefore describe outcomes at below-recommended usage. What happens at full recommended usage is not yet known.

Flagged Patterns & Anomalies
Patterns visible only when subgroups are read together
These patterns come from looking across subgroups rather than reading each table on its own. Each one states what the results show and the questions they raise for discussion. These are not causal claims — this evaluation can identify relationships, but it cannot establish that the product caused them.6
1
The average hides the pattern: the clearest results are among students with the lowest prior performance
What the data shows
A summary report would show one district-wide result: a statistically significant positive relationship. Read by prior-performance group, that single result separates into a much more uneven picture. The relationship is statistically significant only for the two lowest groups9 — roughly the bottom 40% of students by prior performance. For the middle two groups it is not statistically significant. For the highest group the estimate points slightly negative, but it is also not statistically significant, which means this evaluation cannot distinguish it from no relationship at all.
Group 1 (lowest) · statistically significant Group 2 · statistically significant Group 3 · not significant Group 4 · not significant Group 5 (highest) · not significant
Why it stands out
This runs opposite to what is often expected of an adaptive product. The clearest results sit with the students furthest behind, and they disappear entirely at the top of the prior-performance range. Reporting only the district-wide result would show none of this. Importantly, the absence of a significant result for higher-performing students is not evidence that the product harmed them — it means no relationship could be measured in either direction.
Questions for discussion
Is the product's adaptive difficulty better matched to students who are working to catch up? For students who have already mastered grade-level content, is there enough challenge available in the product for a relationship to be detectable at all? Would a separate look at what higher-performing students actually encounter in the product help explain the difference?
2
Special Education: two gaps compound, leaving the result impossible to interpret
What the data shows
Students with a Special Education designation averaged 332 minutes of use, compared with 507 minutes for General Education students, and 13.6% reached the recommended usage compared with 30.3% of General Education students. Their result shows no statistically significant relationship between usage and outcomes, while the General Education result is statistically significant and positive.
Special Education · not statistically significant General Education · statistically significant 332 minutes compared with 507 13.6% compared with 30.3% reaching recommended usage
Why it stands out
Usage and outcomes are normally reported separately, and on its own each of these looks like a minor difference. Together they compound. The group with the least exposure to the product is also the group with no measurable relationship — and because those two things occur together, this evaluation design cannot separate them. The result should be read as unanswered, not as evidence that the product does not work for these students.
Questions for discussion
What is contributing to the difference in usage — scheduling, required accommodations, device access, or how the product is introduced in specialized settings? Would a future evaluation be able to answer the effectiveness question for this group only after usage is more comparable?
3
More usage did not correspond to a stronger result: a reverse pattern
What the data shows
Black or African American students were the only group to clearly exceed the recommended usage, averaging 690 minutes against a 665-minute recommendation — and their result was not statistically significant. Meanwhile White students and students of Two or More Races both averaged below the recommendation, around 490 minutes, and both results were statistically significant.
Black or African American · 690 minutes · not significant White · 491 minutes · statistically significant Two or More Races · 494 minutes · statistically significant
Why it stands out
The rest of this brief rests on the idea that more time using the product is associated with better outcomes. That pattern does not hold here. It suggests total minutes may not capture what matters about how that time is spent. One caution: this group's sample (73 students) is considerably smaller than the White student group's (374), so the estimate is less precise — a smaller sample makes a relationship harder to detect even when one exists.
Questions for discussion
Is time being spent differently — on different lesson difficulty, different completion rates, or with different levels of adult support? Would a measure of how the product is used, rather than only how long, give a clearer picture for every group?
4
Small sample sizes create blind spots for the smallest student groups
What the data shows
The largest single effect size in this brief belongs to students of Two or More Races. It is based on 41 students — close to the 30-student threshold below which results are considered too unstable to report.4 Three further groups — American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, and Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander — fell below that threshold and produced no result at all.
Two or More Races · 41 students Three groups below the reporting threshold
Why it stands out
It would be easy to lead with the largest number in the brief. Doing so would overstate what 41 students can support: as LearnPlatform's own guidance puts it, a moderate result with a narrow range of uncertainty is more trustworthy than a large one with a wide range. More importantly, this brief is quietest about the smallest student populations — which is precisely where an equity review needs the most visibility.
Questions for discussion
Could data be combined across a longer time window or additional grade levels so these groups rise above the reporting threshold? What would it take for a future brief to report on these students rather than suppress their results?

What this section shows, and how to read it
A subgroup is a defined group of students — by grade, gender, race or ethnicity, Special Education status, or prior performance. Looking at results one subgroup at a time answers a question the district-wide number cannot: did this product show the same relationship for every group of students, or only for some?
Read the Significance column first. It tells you whether a result is distinguishable from no relationship at all.2 Only then does the effect size tell you how large that relationship is.3 A result marked "not statistically significant" means this evaluation could not detect a relationship in either direction — it is not evidence that the product failed for that group. Where a group's sample was too small to produce a stable estimate, the row reads "Not plotted" rather than showing an unreliable number.4

Data Notes & Flags
Known gaps and interpretation cautions · Flagged, not silently resolved
  • Two different sample sizes appear in this brief, and both are correct. Effect-size tables use students with both usage data and an outcome score (n = 572 overall); dosage and fidelity tables use all active users (552). These are different populations for different questions and are not reconciled into a single figure.
  • Four race and ethnicity groups are under-reported because of small samples. Three fell below the 30-student threshold and produced no estimate; a fourth sits just above it. Flag 4 covers what this means for reading the brief. Usage data is still reported for all of them.
  • Prior-performance bands do not carry usage figures. Average minutes and dosage attainment are not broken out by prior-performance band in the source data, so the equity finding in Flag 1 cannot yet be checked against whether those students also used the product more. This is a gap to close in the next reporting window.
  • One visual-ambiguity flag from extraction remains on record. A combined ethnicity row in the original worked-example export was flagged because effect-size dot-plot leader lines could not be cleanly resolved. This brief uses the reconciled subgroup table, which separates those groups and marks small-n subgroups as "Not Plotted" instead of inferring values.
  • Correlational design — no causal claim is made or supported. Every finding in this brief describes an association between usage and outcomes. Alternative explanations, including that more motivated or better-supported students both use the product more and score higher, are not ruled out by this design.
Notes & Definitions
Every numbered reference in this brief, in plain language
1This is an example document
This brief is an illustrative sample created to demonstrate report format and structure. The district, school, product, and every figure shown are for demonstration only and do not represent any real district, student population, or product evaluation.
2Statistical significance
Whether a result is likely real rather than a product of chance. This is the first thing to check — before asking how large a relationship is, it is necessary to be confident there is one at all. "Not statistically significant" means no relationship could be detected in either direction. It does not mean the product failed.
3Effect size
A measure of how large a relationship is, once significance has established that there is one. Effect size is useful context, but it cannot stand alone: a moderate effect size with strong significance and a narrow range of uncertainty is more trustworthy than a large one with a wide range. Effect sizes in this brief are partial correlations, the standard measure used in LearnPlatform evaluations.
4Number of students (sample size)
How many students a result is based on. Larger groups produce more reliable estimates. Results based on fewer than 30 students are not reported in this brief — they are marked "Not plotted" rather than shown as an unstable number. A small group makes a relationship harder to detect even when one exists.
5ESSA evidence tiers
A four-level federal framework under the Every Student Succeeds Act describing how strong the evidence behind an intervention is: Level I (Strong), Level II (Moderate), Level III (Promising), and Level IV (Demonstrates a Rationale). The tier reflects the study design, not how well the product performed.
6Association, not cause
This evaluation observed students using a product in real classrooms; it did not assign students to use it. So it can show that greater usage and higher scores occurred together, but not that one caused the other. Other explanations — for example, that more engaged or better-supported students both use the product more and score higher — are not ruled out.
7Recommended usage
The amount of use the product's publisher recommends for the results they describe — here, 665 minutes over 25 weeks, roughly 30 minutes per week. It is a benchmark for interpreting results, not a district requirement or a performance target.
8Likely range (confidence interval)
Rather than a single pinpoint, a range of values likely to contain the true result — a target zone rather than a dot. A narrow range means more precision. If the range crosses zero, the result is not statistically significant, because the true value could be positive, negative, or nothing at all.
9Prior performance groups
For this analysis, students were ranked by their math scores before the evaluation period and divided into five equally sized groups. Group 1 contains the fifth of students with the lowest prior scores; Group 5 contains the fifth with the highest. The groups describe where students started, not how they performed on the outcome assessment.
