RCE overview

This brief evaluates ALEKS across grades 4 through 8 in Sample School District B over the 2023–24 school year, covering 617 students across two schools. Unlike a single-grade, single-school engagement, this one spans five grade levels, giving a wider (and noisier) picture. The design is correlational — usage (topics attempted) is compared against NWEA MAP outcomes, controlling for school and several district-specific covariates (a Hispanic/non-Hispanic flag, Special Education status, Title I status, and a program/team grouping). This is the first Research Brief for this engagement, so it serves as the baseline for future comparison.

Flagged patterns & anomalies
These patterns surfaced from scanning across every subgroup and usage metric at once. Each states what the data shows, why it stands out, and the question it raises — never a causal claim.
1
A large equity gap in usage compliance by Title I status

Among Title I students, only 1 of 48 (about 2%) met the usage goal. Among non-Title I students, 176 of 515 (about 34%) did.

Title I: 2% met goal (n=48) Non-Title I: 34% met goal (n=515)

This is a much wider gap than a typical subgroup difference, roughly a seventeen-fold difference in compliance rate, not a modest few-percentage-point gap.

What's different about how Title I students are accessing or being assigned ALEKS in this district? Is this a device or access issue, a scheduling issue, or something in how the program is rolled out at the school(s) serving more Title I students?

2
The only significant subgroup effect is among the highest prior-performance students, the opposite pattern from other engagements

Of five prior-performance bands, only the highest (Group 5) shows a statistically significant positive effect. The four lower bands are all non-significant, with two trending slightly negative.

Group 5 (highest): r = +0.20, p = 0.042, n = 113 Groups 1–4: not significant

In the Southeast Polk ALEKS brief, the strongest, clearest effects were concentrated in the lowest prior-performance bands. Here it's reversed. With p = 0.042 and a fairly wide interval, this is a real but not a strongly robust finding, and it doesn't tell the same "helps students furthest behind" story seen elsewhere.

Is there something about how higher-performing students in this district use ALEKS differently, and should this change how we frame this product's evidence story for this district versus others?

3
School-level effect sits right at the edge of significance

The larger of the two schools (391 students) has an effect size of +0.11 with a 95% CI of −0.001 to 0.21, a hair's-breadth from excluding zero.

Results this close to the significance threshold are fragile. A small change in sample or model specification could flip this either way.

Would a follow-up period, or a slightly larger sample, resolve this one way or the other?

Appendix — data notes & flags
  • ESSA tier not established at the district level. The overall effect doesn't reach statistical significance, so no district-wide evidence tier is claimed here.
  • "BGC HA" is an undefined category. It appears in the "Team" dimension with only 6 students and no available definition in the source data. Flagging rather than guessing what it stands for.
  • Cosmetic label quirk in the source system. Grade-level labels in the raw data read "4rd_grade," "6rd_grade," etc. (should be "4th," "6th"). This doesn't affect any number, just the label text.
  • Small-n subgroups not plotted. Asian (n=3), Black or African American (n=2), and the "BGC HA" team category (n=6) are all below the n=30 threshold for a stable effect-size estimate.
  • Two different sample sizes appear across tables, and that's expected. The effect-size table uses the population with both usage and an outcome score (e.g., 617 district-wide). The usage-compliance figures use the broader active-user population (e.g., 575 district-wide). See shared/prompts/db_field_source_map.md §6 for why these legitimately differ.
