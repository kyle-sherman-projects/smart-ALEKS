# ALEKS outcomes analysis — Rapid Cycle Evaluation summary for Sample School District B

**Reporting period:** August 14, 2023 – April 12, 2024
**Grade levels:** 4th–8th grade
**Schools:** two schools in-district (labeled School 1 and School 2 below)
**Outcome measure:** NWEA MAP
**Usage metric:** Topics Attempted (goal: 284 topics)
**Data baseline:** No prior brief exists for this district in this repository. This report is the baseline.

---

## RCE overview

This brief evaluates ALEKS across grades 4 through 8 in Sample School District B over the 2023–24 school year, covering 617 students. Unlike a single-grade, single-school engagement, this one spans five grade levels and two schools, giving a wider (and noisier) picture. The design is correlational — usage (topics attempted) is compared against NWEA MAP outcomes, controlling for school and several district-specific covariates (Hispanic/non-Hispanic flag, Special Education status, Title I status, and a program/team grouping).

## Summary of impacts

Across all 617 students, more time spent on ALEKS topics showed a small positive association with NWEA MAP scores (r = +0.08), but the 95% confidence interval (−0.01 to 0.16) just barely crosses zero, so this result doesn't meet the standard bar for statistical significance. In plain terms: the data leans positive, but we can't rule out that this pattern is due to chance.

Usage compliance was low: only about 31% of actively-using students reached the 284-topic goal. That's a similar usage shortfall to other ALEKS engagements we've reviewed, but here it comes without a clearly significant overall benefit to point to as the upside of closing that gap.

---

## Flagged patterns & anomalies

### 1. A large equity gap in usage compliance by Title I status

**What the data shows:** among Title I students, only 1 of 48 (about 2%) met the usage goal. Among non-Title I students, 176 of 515 (about 34%) did.

**Why it stands out:** this is a much wider gap than a typical subgroup difference — roughly a seventeen-fold difference in compliance rate, not a modest few-percentage-point gap.

**The question it raises:** what's different about how Title I students are accessing or being assigned ALEKS in this district? Is this a device/access issue, a scheduling issue, or something in how the program is rolled out at the school(s) serving more Title I students?

### 2. The only significant subgroup effect is among the highest prior-performance students — the opposite pattern from other engagements

**What the data shows:** of the five prior-performance bands, only the highest (Group 5) shows a statistically significant positive effect (r = +0.20, 95% CI: 0.01–0.38, p = 0.042, n = 113). The four lower bands are all non-significant, with two trending slightly negative.

**Why it stands out:** in the Southeast Polk ALEKS brief, the strongest, clearest effects were concentrated in the *lowest* prior-performance bands. Here it's reversed. Worth being cautious about this one: with p = 0.042 and a fairly wide interval, it's a real but not a strongly robust finding, and it doesn't tell the same "helps students furthest behind" story we've seen elsewhere.

**The question it raises:** is there something about how higher-performing students in this district use ALEKS differently, and should this change how we frame this product's evidence story for this specific district versus others?

### 3. School-level effect is right at the edge of significance

**What the data shows:** the larger of the two schools (391 students) has an effect size of +0.11 with a 95% CI of −0.001 to 0.21, a hair's-breadth from excluding zero.

**Why it stands out:** results this close to the significance threshold are fragile. A small change in sample or model specification could flip this either way.

**The question it raises:** would a follow-up period (or a slightly larger sample) resolve this one way or the other?

## Next steps — discussion agenda

1. **Title I usage compliance.** Why did only about 2% of Title I students meet the usage goal, versus about 34% of non-Title I students? *(Flag 1)*
2. **Borderline overall result.** The district-wide effect is close to, but doesn't reach, statistical significance. Would a longer observation window or larger sample resolve this? *(Summary of impacts)*
3. **Reversed prior-performance pattern.** Why does the significant effect here concentrate in the highest-performing band, rather than the lowest, as seen in other ALEKS engagements? *(Flag 2)*
4. **The undefined "Team" grouping.** What does the "BGC HA" category represent, and is it worth tracking separately going forward? *(Appendix D)*

## Appendix — data notes & flags

- **ESSA tier not established at the district level.** The overall effect doesn't reach statistical significance, so no district-wide evidence tier is claimed here (see the note under "Overview of measures").
- **"BGC HA" is an undefined category.** It appears in the `cov5` ("Team") dimension with only 6 students and no available definition in the source data. Flagging rather than guessing what it stands for.
- **Cosmetic label quirk in the source system.** Grade-level labels in the raw data read `"4rd_grade"`, `"6rd_grade"`, etc. (should be "4th," "6th"). This doesn't affect any number, just the label text.
- **Small-n subgroups not plotted.** Asian (n=3), Black or African American (n=2), and the "BGC HA" team category (n=6) are all below the n=30 threshold for a stable effect-size estimate.
- **Two different sample sizes appear across tables, and that's expected.** The effect-size table uses the population with both usage and an outcome score (e.g., 617 district-wide, 410 for White students). The usage-compliance table uses the broader active-user population (e.g., 575 district-wide, 383 for White students). See `shared/prompts/db_field_source_map.md` §6 for why these legitimately differ.
